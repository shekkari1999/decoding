Lessons I Learned by Implementing Speculative Decoding

I wanted to find out whether speculative decoding could deliver a real performance improvement. Building it step by step taught me how probability distributions, KV caches, and a few extra model calls shape the result.

September 27, 2026 · Notes from my PyTorch, Transformers, and vLLM experiments

I started with a question: how much faster can a large language model generate if a smaller model proposes its next few tokens?

The idea sounded straightforward. Let a small model draft several tokens, then let the large model check them together. I wanted to understand the math, build my own implementation, and measure the difference.

I worked through this in spec-decode.ipynb. I began with individual model calls, added one speculative round, inspected it in the debugger, and then built a loop that could generate a complete response.

The path I followed
  1. Understand the sampling math
  2. Measure each model separately
  3. Inspect one round in the debugger
  4. Get the KV caches right
  5. Try a larger target model
  6. Remove an extra target pass
  7. Compare the results

First, Understand What We Are Sampling

I focused on speculative sampling, the version where tokens are sampled from probability distributions. The large model is the target. The smaller model is the draft.

At each position, the target assigns probabilities to possible next tokens. Call that distribution p. The draft has its own distribution, q. The goal is to use the draft's cheaper proposals while preserving the target's probabilities.

If the target gives a token a 30% probability, the combined algorithm should still give it a 30% probability. This is what makes the acceptance and correction rules important.

Why accept with min(1, p/q)?

The draft samples a token x. We accept it using:

α(x) = min(1, p(x) / q(x))

Suppose the draft assigns a token 60% probability and the target assigns it 30%. The draft proposes it twice as often as the target wants. Accepting half those proposals brings the accepted probability mass down to 30%.

When the target gives a proposal at least as much probability as the draft does, we accept it every time.

Why sample from the residual after rejection?

This was one of the most useful parts of the math for me. Accepted proposals already account for some of the target's probability mass. A correction needs to fill the remaining mass.

correction = normalize(max(p − q, 0))

Here is a small example:

TokenDraft qTarget pResidual mass
A0.600.300.00
B0.300.400.10
C0.100.300.20

The accepted branch contributes 0.30 for A, 0.30 for B, and 0.10 for C. That leaves 0.10 for B and 0.20 for C. After a rejection, the normalized residual selects B with probability 1/3 and C with probability 2/3.

The accepted branch and the correction branch together recover the target's distribution. This also explains why I had to keep the draft's full distribution at every position. The probability of the selected token is enough for acceptance; correction needs the probabilities of every token.

Measure Each Model Separately

Before combining the models, I downloaded them from Hugging Face and ran each one individually. This gave me a baseline and helped me understand how much cheaper the draft actually was.

I started with Qwen experiments, including Qwen3 and Qwen2.5. The notebook retains the Qwen2.5-7B target and Qwen2.5-0.5B draft paths from that earlier work. Those early experiments gave me little benefit, and the speculative sampler could be slower than target-only generation.

One comparison detail needed fixing early: my individual model calls used greedy decoding, while my speculative code sampled tokens. I changed the baselines to use the same sampling policy:

do_sample=True,
temperature=1.0,
top_k=0,
top_p=1.0

These settings sample from the full next-token distribution. I warmed up the models before measuring, synchronized CUDA around the timing window, and recorded both generated token counts and elapsed time.

Watch One Round Happen

I built a single round before writing the full generation loop. The debugger let me stop after drafting, after target verification, and at each acceptance decision.

1. DraftSample a few tokens from q.
2. VerifyEvaluate the block with the target.
3. CompareInspect p(x), q(x), and the random draw.
4. CommitKeep accepted tokens and a correction or bonus.

I could inspect the proposed tokens, their probabilities under both models, and the full distributions used for correction. Seeing those values made the equations easier to connect to the code.

Acceptance proceeds from left to right. After the first rejected token, every later proposal is discarded because the draft generated it using a prefix that included that rejected token.

If all proposals pass, the target can supply a bonus token. A round with k proposals can therefore emit up to k + 1 tokens.

The logit alignment detail

A model's output after consuming a token predicts the following token. The final prompt logit predicts proposal one. The logit after consuming proposal one predicts proposal two. Getting this shift right was essential when comparing the two distributions.

I also checked that the tokenizers mapped the same token strings to the same IDs, calculated softmax probabilities in FP32, and used a seeded generator to reproduce acceptance and rejection paths.

KV Caches Were the Hardest Implementation Detail

Both models keep a KV cache containing attention state for tokens they have already processed. Reusing that state saves us from processing the entire prefix again on every call.

Speculation makes cache management more involved. The target processes the whole proposal block before we know how much of it will be accepted. A rejection means some of that cached state must be removed.

For example, if the draft proposes four tokens and only the first two pass, the target cache must discard the last two. The correction then becomes part of the committed sequence.

x₁ accepted x₂ accepted x₃ rejected x₄ discarded y correction

The draft had its own subtle detail: after sampling its final proposal, it had yet to feed that token back into the model. Four sampled proposals meant three new entries in its cache. I had to account for that difference when rolling back or catching up.

I added cache-length assertions and walked through rejection, full acceptance, EOS stopping, and output-length boundaries. Those checks helped me trust the generation loop before measuring its speed.

Try a Larger Gap Between Target and Draft

After the early Qwen results, I wanted to try a much larger target with a relatively small draft. I moved to Llama 3.1 70B Instruct and Llama 3.1 8B Instruct.

That is roughly a ninefold difference in parameter count. The useful measurement was the difference in generation cost: the 70B target ran at about 9.6 tokens/s, while the 8B draft ran at about 41 tokens/s.

I used BF16 weights on two RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs. The download initially included duplicate original-format weights, so I restricted it to the safetensors checkpoints and checked that all shards and tokenizer files were complete.

The first Llama speculative run reached 11.2 tokens/s, with 69.8% acceptance. That looked like a modest gain. The token lengths differed between that run and the earlier baseline, so I treated it as an early observation.

The pass count was more revealing: 512 generated tokens took 333 target calls across 166 rounds. My code was doing nearly two target passes per round.

Remove an Extra Target Pass

My first implementation verified the proposal block, then made another target call to process the correction or bonus token. That second call kept the target cache up to date, but it also added expensive work.

We changed the loop to save that emitted token as a pending token. On the next round, the target consumes it together with the next proposal block:

verification_input = [pending_token, proposal_1, proposal_2, proposal_3]

The draft cache contains every committed token. The target cache stays one token behind until the next verification call. After consuming the pending token, the target's first output predicts the first new proposal.

One target call per round: the correction or bonus travels with the next verification block. The target also makes one initial prefill call.

We checked the change against the original function using small context-dependent CPU models. Across 240 cases, the revised function produced identical tokens and acceptance decisions. Those cases covered corrections, bonus tokens, EOS, and length limits.

The revised Llama run reached 16.1 tokens/s. Its 39 rounds used 40 target calls, which confirmed the intended reduction. The gain came from doing less target work while keeping the sampling rules intact.

What the Measurements Showed

I also wanted to see how the same model pair performed in vLLM. We ran the official vLLM 0.30.0 Docker image, split the target across both GPUs, and used probabilistic drafting with three proposals per round.

The first result was surprising: target-only generation reached 19.8 tokens/s, while speculation reached 6.7 tokens/s. Looking at the logs exposed a benchmark configuration issue. CUDA graphs covered one and two tokens, while verification with three proposals could require four.

We added graph sizes [1, 2, 4] and extended warmup to a full 512-token generation. The rerun reached 43.1 tokens/s with speculation, compared with 19.8 tokens/s for the target alone: 2.17×. Both changes happened together, so their individual effects still need a separate comparison.

For my own sampler, I later clarified the prompt to ask about speculative decoding for large language model inference. I also returned only generated text and handled all three Llama stop tokens. I tried the chat template, then returned to plain prompts.

The latest plain-prompt notebook results were:

The natural responses had different lengths: 84 target-only tokens and 106 speculative tokens. The fixed 128-token run gives the cleaner throughput comparison. These are individual measurements; repeated runs and more prompts will help establish how much the gain varies.

For the blog, my takeaway is that the implementation achieved about 1.5× speedup on a matched fixed-length run. The vLLM experiment showed a larger gain for the same model pair under its own configuration.

A few things I would check earlier next time

  • Match the sampling policy. Keep temperature, token filtering, and EOS rules consistent between baseline and speculative runs.
  • Count target calls. This exposed the extra call in my implementation.
  • Inspect cache state. Track tokens consumed by each model separately from tokens sampled.
  • Measure acceptance alongside cost. A high acceptance rate helps when drafting and verification are cheap enough.
  • Check model placement. CPU offloading and leftover models in the notebook kernel can change timings.
  • Separate response demos from benchmarks. EOS stopping gives natural answers; fixed lengths make speed comparisons easier.

What I Want to Explore Next

I now have a working sampler and a measurable gain. The next steps are to repeat the matched benchmarks, try more prompts, and investigate the remaining Python and GPU synchronization overhead.

I also want to explore Medusa and EAGLE. Building this implementation gives me a clearer starting point for understanding how other approaches propose tokens and manage verification.

The most useful lesson came from watching the algorithm run. The probability math explained how to preserve the target distribution. The debugger showed where those probabilities entered the code. The cache checks kept the context consistent. Counting target calls showed me where performance was being spent.

That is the part I enjoyed most: each improvement became easier to reason about once I could see exactly what the models were doing.
